## 5 facts

1. **Brazzaville in 1890 was a young French post on a Teke village site.** It was founded on 10 September 1880 on the site of Nkuna, a Bateke village (the area was also known as Mfoa), after the Makoko of the Teke signed a treaty with Brazza. — [BlackPast: Brazzaville, Congo (1880–)](https://blackpast.org/global-african-history/brazzaville-congo-1880/), [African Heritage: Why the name Brazzaville?](https://afrolegends.com/2014/06/10/why-the-name-brazzaville/). *Confidence: medium (search summary).*

2. **Dress around the Pool mixed local raffia cloth with imported trade cloth and ready-made items.** Among the Tio (Teke), "elaborate raphia cloths, jewellery and special insignia conveyed membership in dominant families"; scenes of daily life show people "dressed in imported pagnes and ready-made items such as shirts, hats, and jackets". Military caps and top hats were popular among chiefs, interpreters and traders (men). — Phyllis M. Martin, "Contesting Clothes in Colonial Brazzaville", *Journal of African History* 35:3 (1994), 401–426 ([Cambridge abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-african-history/article/abs/contesting-clothes-in-colonial-brazzaville1/C9670101C8B57D0937722EEEB5433891), [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/182642.pdf)). *Confidence: medium — quotes come from search summaries of the article, full text not read. Most clothing detail found concerns men and elites; what an ordinary woman wore is not established.*

3. **A pagne is a length of cloth worn wrapped.** "A pagne is normally fabric cut into a 2-by-6 yard piece"; the word, from French, "has come to refer to any cloth for everyday wear". — [Wrapper (clothing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrapper_(clothing)) via search summary. *Confidence: medium. How women around the Pool wrapped it in 1890 (waist or chest, with or without a top) was not found → `[TODO: ask Marty]`.*

4. **Teke brass neck rings signalled wealth.** Teke metalworkers made flat brass neck rings, dated 19th–20th century, "worn as a symbol of wealth". — [The Met: Collar, Teke peoples](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/314993), [Teke people](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teke_people). *Confidence: medium. Adding one would make her a wealthy woman — a biographical choice, so it's left out of the prompt unless Marty chooses it.*

5. **An 1890 print would almost certainly be an albumen print: warm brown, not neutral grey.** Albumen paper dominated from 1855 until about 1890–1895; gelatin silver had "by 1895 … generally replaced albumen prints". Albumen prints have a characteristic warm, sepia-brown tone. — [The Albumen & Salted Paper Book, App. C](https://cool.culturalheritage.org/albumen/library/monographs/reilly/app-c.html), [PSAP photographic prints guide](https://psap.library.illinois.edu/collection-id-guide/photoprint), [Cabinet card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabinet_card). *Confidence: high (several consistent sources).*

## Studio conventions (context, not a fact about Brazzaville)
- West African studios followed Victorian conventions: "sitters posed formally before the customary painted backdrop", with props such as balustrades, curtains, pillars and chairs; "by the early 1900s, most West African capitals had permanent studios". — [The Met: Early Histories of Photography in West Africa (1860–1910)](https://metmuseum.org/toah/hd/ephwa/hd_ephwa.htm). *Confidence: medium.*
- Photographers active in Central Africa then included the Lutterodt family (Gabon, Congo, from the 1870s) and H. A. Shanu (a studio at Boma, Congo Free State); Robert Visser photographed the Loango coast c. 1890–1900. — [The Met essay](https://metmuseum.org/toah/hd/ephwa/hd_ephwa.htm), [Herzekiah Andrew Shanu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herzekiah_Andrew_Shanu), [Robert Visser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Visser).
